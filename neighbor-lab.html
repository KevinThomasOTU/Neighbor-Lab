How the Doodle Teacher works

Your drawing is cropped to fit, shrunk onto a 28 × 28 grid (the same size as the MNIST handwritten-digit images, LeCun et al., 1998), and lightly blurred so tiny wobbles matter less. That gives 784 numbers. The model measures the straight-line (Euclidean) distance between your 784 numbers and those of every example you taught, then the k closest examples vote. This is k-nearest neighbors (Fix & Hodges, 1951; Cover & Hart, 1967).

It has no idea what a sun or a house is. It only compares pixels, so a rotated or very lopsided drawing can fool it, especially with only a few examples. More, and more varied, training examples usually help. Real systems like Teachable Machine use richer features learned by neural networks, but the “compare to examples” idea is the same.

The example doodles are drawn by a small program with random wobble, not by people.

How the Neighbor Map works

Each animal has two features: weight (0–40 kg) and ear pointiness (0–10). Both are rescaled to a 0–1 range before measuring distance; otherwise kilograms would drown out ears. The animals are simulated, not real measurements. 60 animals are used for training (filled shapes) and 40 are held back for testing (hollow shapes). About 7% of labels are deliberately wrong, the way real datasets contain mistakes.

With k = 1, every training animal is its own nearest neighbor, so training accuracy is 100%. The test score is the honest measure. Watch the gap between the two lines: a big gap means overfitting, and both lines dropping at large k means underfitting (James et al., 2021, Ch. 2; Google Machine Learning Crash Course).
